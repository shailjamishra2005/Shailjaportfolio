Netflix Pricing Strategy

How one of the world's most studied subscription businesses uses price discrimination to serve every kind of viewer while protecting its bottom line.

Business Economics Price Discrimination Consumer Behaviour Brand Strategy
Project Details
TypeCase analysis · Written document

The Question

Why does Netflix charge differently for the same content?

Netflix operates in a market where the same show is watched by a student in a hostel and an executive on a flight. They value the product differently. They will pay differently. The question this case asked was simple: how does Netflix know this, and what does it do about it?

The answer sits in the economics of price discrimination — charging different consumers different prices for a product that costs the same to deliver. Netflix has built its entire pricing architecture around this principle without most of its users ever realising it.

The Tiers

Four plans. Four types of consumer.

Mobile

₹149

One screen, 480p. Price-sensitive users who watch only on phones or tablets.

Basic

₹199

One screen, 720p HD. Solo viewers who want TV access on a budget.

Standard

₹499

Two screens, 1080p Full HD. The anchor plan — designed to make Premium feel like reasonable value.

Premium

₹649

Four screens, 4K Ultra HD. Low-elasticity households willing to pay for quality and simultaneous access.

Key Insights

What the pricing reveals about the strategy

01

Demand is not uniform

Different user segments have fundamentally different willingness to pay. Netflix segments by device, screen count and resolution rather than identity — which is smarter and less intrusive.

02

The mobile plan is a market entry tool

At ₹149 the mobile plan is not built to maximise revenue per user. It is built to maximise market penetration in price-sensitive demographics and create long-term brand loyalty.

03

Anchoring drives upgrades

The Standard plan at ₹499 exists to make the ₹649 Premium look reasonable by comparison. This is textbook price anchoring used to extract surplus from higher-income households.

04

Content is the real moat

Pricing strategy only works if consumers believe the product is worth it. Netflix's investment in original content sustains the perceived value that justifies its premium tiers in a competitive market.

"The best pricing strategy is invisible to the consumer but deeply felt by the business."

The Framework

Economics behind the decision

The case applied third-degree price discrimination — where a firm charges different prices to identifiable market segments based on their demand elasticity. Students and budget users have high elasticity, meaning price changes strongly affect their behaviour. Premium households have low elasticity and will pay more without switching off.

This is paired with profit maximisation theory — each tier is priced at a point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost for that segment, extracting maximum value from each consumer group without losing them entirely to competitors like Prime Video or Hotstar.

Key Takeaway

Netflix is not selling subscriptions. It is selling the same content at different prices to different people who all believe they got a fair deal. That is what good pricing strategy looks like from the inside.
